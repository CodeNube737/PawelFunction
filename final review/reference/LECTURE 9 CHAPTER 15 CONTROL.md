## Pages 1–3: Introduction to Control

- **Control (definition):** A regulatory process of establishing standards to achieve organizational goals, comparing actual performance to those standards, and taking corrective action when necessary.
- **Standards:** The specific benchmarks or criteria used to determine whether performance is satisfactory or unsatisfactory.
- **Benchmarking:** Determining how well other organizations (not only direct competitors) perform particular functions or tasks, and using their practices/standards as a reference point.

**Key ideas:**
- Control is ongoing and tied directly to organizational goals.
- Without clear standards, it is impossible to know whether performance is acceptable.
- Benchmarking helps organizations avoid “inward-looking” standards by learning from others.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 1–3)**
- Q1: In your own words, why is control necessary in organizations?
	- A1: Control keeps activities aligned with goals, lets managers detect problems early, and ensures resources are used effectively instead of drifting off course.
- Q2: How do “standards” differ from “benchmarking”?
	- A2: Standards are the specific performance targets set inside the organization; benchmarking is comparing your performance or practices to other organizations’ best practices or results.
- Q3: Give one example of a standard and one example of a benchmark from a company or work experience you know.
	- A3: Example standard: “Answer 90% of customer calls within 30 seconds.” Example benchmark: “Our on-time delivery rate should match or exceed the industry leader’s 98% on-time rate.”

## Pages 4–7: The Control Process

- **Control process (steps):**
	- Establishing standards.
	- Comparing actual performance to standards.
	- Taking corrective action when deviations occur.
- **Dynamic, cybernetic process:** Control is continuous, self-correcting, and ongoing, rather than a one-time event.
- **Control loss:** When behaviour or work procedures do not conform to standards.
- **Corrective action steps:**
	- Identify performance deviations.
	- Analyze deviations.
	- Develop and implement corrective programs.
**Key ideas:**
- Effective control requires accurate measurement and information systems to compare actual performance with standards.
- Control is not just about punishment; it is about detecting deviations and making improvements.
- The process is “cybernetic” because feedback from results is used to adjust behaviour and processes.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 4–7)**
- Q4: List the three main steps in the control process.
	- A4: (1) Establish standards, (2) compare actual performance to those standards, (3) take corrective action when there are deviations.
- Q5: What is “control loss,” and why is it a problem?
	- A5: Control loss occurs when actual behaviour and procedures no longer match the standards; it’s a problem because performance becomes unpredictable and goals are less likely to be achieved.
- Q6: Suppose a team consistently misses deadlines. Walk through the corrective action steps for this situation.
	- A6: Identify that deadlines are consistently missed; analyze causes (unrealistic estimates, bottlenecks, poor communication); develop and implement solutions such as better scheduling, more resources, clearer responsibilities, or process changes, then monitor whether deadlines are now met.

## Page 8–10: Types of Control & When Control Is Worth It

- **Types of control (timing):**
	- **Feedforward control:** Monitoring inputs and conditions _before_ they occur to prevent problems (e.g., screening suppliers, pre-employment testing).
	- **Concurrent control:** Monitoring activities _as they occur_ to allow immediate corrections (e.g., supervisors on the shop floor, real-time dashboards).
	- **Feedback control:** Using information about _after-the-fact_ results to correct future performance (e.g., year-end financial statements).
- **Control loss (revisited):** Behaviour and work procedures do not conform to standards.
- **Regulation costs:** Costs of implementing or maintaining control systems (e.g., audits, monitoring tools, time spent on reporting).
- **Cybernetic feasibility:** The extent to which it is possible to implement all steps in the control process (measurement, feedback, correction) in a practical and affordable way.

**Key ideas:**
- Not all control systems are worth their cost; if regulation costs exceed expected benefits, control is not worthwhile.
- Organizations should match the type of control (feedforward, concurrent, feedback) to the nature of the activity and available information.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 8–10)**
- Q7: Describe a situation from school or work where feedforward control would be useful.
	- A7: For example, before a group project starts, selecting team members with the required skills and clarifying expectations is feedforward control because it tries to prevent problems before work begins.
- Q8: Give one example each of concurrent and feedback control.
	- A8: Concurrent: a supervisor watching a production line and making adjustments in real time. Feedback: using exam results at the end of the semester to adjust teaching for the next semester.
- Q9: Why might a theoretically good control system be impossible to implement (low cybernetic feasibility)?
	- A9: It may be too expensive, difficult to measure performance accurately, or too complex to gather timely information and take corrective action, so the full loop of measurement–comparison–correction can’t be practically implemented.

## Pages 11–13: Control Methods & Balanced Scorecard

- **Control methods:**
	- **Bureaucratic control:** Use of rules, policies, hierarchy, and authority to influence behaviour.
	- **Objective control:** Use of observable measures of worker behaviour or outputs to assess performance.
	- **Normative control:** Shaping behaviour by instilling a strong organizational culture and shared values.
	- **Concertive control:** Control based on beliefs and norms that are developed and enforced by work groups themselves.
	- **Self-control:** Individuals set their own goals, monitor their own progress, and reward themselves for achieving goals.
- **Balanced Scorecard:** An integrated framework for controlling organizational performance by looking at multiple perspectives:
	- Financial performance.
	- Customer perspective (e.g., customer defections).
	- Internal quality and processes.
	- Innovation and learning (including waste and improvement).

**Key ideas:**
- Different control methods rely on different levers (rules, numbers, culture, peer norms, self-discipline).
- The balanced scorecard prevents managers from focusing only on financial metrics while ignoring customers, quality, and innovation.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 11–13)**
- Q10: How does concertive control differ from bureaucratic control?
	- A10: Bureaucratic control uses formal rules, hierarchy, and top‑down authority, whereas concertive control is created and enforced by the work group itself through shared values and peer pressure.
- Q11: Why is self-control powerful, and what risks might it pose?
	- A11: Self‑control is powerful because people are more committed to goals they set themselves and can react quickly; the risk is that personal goals may drift away from organizational goals or that people under‑ or over‑control themselves.
- Q12: Briefly describe the four perspectives of the balanced scorecard.
	- A12: Financial: how well the organization is doing in financial terms. Customer: how customers see the organization, including defections. Internal: how well key internal processes and quality standards are being managed. Innovation and learning: the organization’s ability to improve, innovate, and adapt over time.

## Pages 14–16: Financial Perspective

- **Cash flow analysis:** Examines how changes in the business affect its ability to take in more cash than it pays out.
- **Balance sheet:** Snapshot of a company’s financial position at a specific point in time (assets, liabilities, equity).
- **Income statement:** Shows revenues, expenses, and net profit over a period (e.g., quarter or year).
- **Financial ratios:** Metrics to assess liquidity (ability to meet short-term obligations), efficiency (asset use), and profitability.
- **Budgets:** Quantitative plans that allocate financial resources to activities.
- **Economic Value Added (EVA):** A measure of financial performance that focuses on whether a company is creating value above the cost of capital.

**Key ideas:**
- Financial controls help answer “Are we creating economic value?” rather than just “Are we profitable?”
- Cash flow, budgets, and ratios provide early warning signs of financial problems.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 14–16)**
- Q13: Why might a profitable company still have cash flow problems?
	- A13: Because profit is an accounting measure over a period, while cash flow reflects actual inflows and outflows; a firm can show a profit but still run short of cash if, for example, customers pay late or inventory ties up cash.
- Q14: What is the main purpose of using financial ratios?
	- A14: To provide standardized indicators that allow managers to track liquidity, efficiency, and profitability, compare performance over time, and benchmark against other firms.
- Q15: Conceptually, what does Economic Value Added (EVA) try to capture that basic profit does not?
	- A15: EVA asks whether the company is earning more than the cost of the capital it uses; it focuses on value created for investors beyond simple accounting profit.

## Pages 17–19: Customer and Internal Perspectives

- **Customer perspective – controlling defections:**
	- Customer surveys can be misleading; customers may report satisfaction yet still switch to competitors.
	- Monitoring customer **defections** (the rate at which customers leave) can be more informative.
	- Businesses often lose 15–20% of their customers annually.
	- Retaining just 5% more customers can increase profit by 25–100%.
- **Internal perspective – controlling quality:** Focuses on what the organization must excel at.
	- **Excellence:** High overall quality, often defined broadly (may be subjective).
	- **Value:** Quality relative to price; customers’ perception that they are getting a good deal.
	- **Conformance to specifications:** Products/services meet precise design or process standards.

**Key ideas:**
- Reducing defections can be more profitable than constantly acquiring new customers.
- Quality can be defined in different ways; what matters most is how it is measured and managed.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 17–19)**
- Q16: Why is tracking customer defections often more useful than relying on satisfaction surveys?
	- A16: Because what customers say on surveys may not match their actual behaviour; defections directly show whether customers stay or leave, which affects revenue and profit.
- Q17: Compare “excellence,” “value,” and “conformance to specifications” as approaches to defining quality.
	- A17: Excellence focuses on very high overall quality (often subjective); value focuses on the trade‑off between quality and price from the customer’s point of view; conformance to specifications focuses on meeting detailed, objective product or process standards.
- Q18: Give an example of a company that focuses on value versus one that focuses on excellence.
	- A18: A luxury brand like Rolex focuses on excellence; a discount retailer like Walmart focuses on value by offering acceptable quality at low prices.

## Pages 20–22: Innovation, Learning, and Waste Reduction

- **Innovation and learning perspective:** Focuses on an organization’s ability to improve, adapt, and innovate over time.
- **Waste prevention and reduction strategies:**
	1. **Good housekeeping:** Basic maintenance, organization, and efficiency practices to reduce waste (e.g., turning off unused equipment, minimizing scrap).
	2. **Material or product substitution:** Using less harmful, less wasteful, or more efficient materials or products.
	3. **Process modification:** Redesigning processes to reduce waste at the source (e.g., lean manufacturing, redesigning workflows).

**Key ideas:**
- Long-term performance depends on innovation and learning, not just short-term financial results.
- Waste reduction strategies can simultaneously lower costs and improve environmental performance.

**Comprehension Q&A (Pages 20–22)**
- Q19: Why is innovation and learning included in the balanced scorecard?
	- A19: Because long‑term success depends on the ability to innovate, learn, and improve processes and products, not just on current financial results; including it keeps managers focused on future capabilities.
- Q20: Briefly explain each of the three waste reduction strategies.
	- A20: Good housekeeping: maintaining clean, orderly, and efficient operations to avoid unnecessary waste. Material/product substitution: choosing less wasteful or less harmful materials or products. Process modification: redesigning processes so they inherently generate less waste.
- Q21: Think of a real or hypothetical process and suggest one practical example of each waste reduction strategy.
	- A21: Example process: a small manufacturing line. Good housekeeping: organizing tools and cleaning spills promptly to reduce scrap. Material substitution: switching to a raw material that produces fewer offcuts. Process modification: redesigning the cutting process to nest parts more efficiently and minimize leftover material.
