## Pages 31-40 Summary

**Limited Partnerships (LP)**

* LPs have limited liability, only liable up to their registered investment, no management authority.
* General Partners (GPs) pay LPs a return (like dividends).
* Only GPs can transact business.
* LPs should not have their name in the partnership name; involvement in management may elevate LPs to GPs.

**Limited Liability Partnerships (LLP)**

* Some or all partners have limited liability.
* Combines elements of partnerships and corporations.
* Partners not liable for another’s misconduct/negligence.
* Some countries require at least one GP with unlimited liability.

**Corporations**

* Separate legal entity (“person”) from shareholders.
* Shareholders own via shares, appoint directors.
* Directors oversee management, appoint officers.
* Officers manage daily operations.
* Formation requires meeting federal/provincial statute requirements.
* Federal corporations: business across Canada; provincial: only in own province.
* Foreign corporations must register to operate in a province.

## Pages 11–20 Summary
 Key GNU Public License (GPL) Terms:
	- If you distribute a program or any “work based on the program,” you must also distribute the source code and license it under GPL terms at no charge.
	- “Work based on the program” includes the program itself and any derivative work (verbatim, modified, or translated).
	- Exceptions: Independent works not derived from GPL software and mere aggregation of unrelated works on the same medium are not subject to GPL.
 Key Lesser General Public License (LGPL) Terms:
	- Similar to GPL, but intended to promote use of certain libraries with non-free programs.
	- Exception: Linking works that use the library to proprietary programs is allowed, reducing Copyleft concerns.
 Copyleft under LGPL:
	- Same Copyleft obligations as GPL, except:
		- Works using only small macros, inline functions, or data structures are not subject to Copyleft.
		- Works designed to work with the library (compiled/linked) but not containing its code are not subject to Copyleft.
 Other Open Source Licenses (BSD/Apache):
	- Permissive approach: Modifications may be proprietary.
	- Distribution in source or object code is allowed if copyright notice and liability disclaimer are included.
	- Contributors’ names cannot be used to endorse products.

- **Introduction to Conflicts of Interest (COI):**
	- COI is a critical topic in professional ethics, especially for organizations and engineers.
	- Good governance and risk management require identifying and managing COI to maintain public trust.

- **Definition of Conflict of Interest:**
	- A COI involves the risk of personal benefit and/or bias.
	- It arises when an employee may benefit personally (or appear to) from dealings with entities or persons doing business with their employer.
	- This includes indirect benefits to family or closely associated businesses.
	- Financial or other considerations may compromise, or appear to compromise, objectivity or professional judgment.

- **Professional Duties:**
	- Professionals have a duty to avoid situations where COI could arise.
	- Managing COI is part of maintaining the integrity and credibility of the profession.

## Summary: Pages 1–10

### 1. Dispute and Conflict Resolution
- Disputes/conflicts arise for many reasons; various resolution methods exist.
- Each method has explicit or implicit rules.

### 2. Common Methods of Resolution
- Consultant engagement
- Negotiation
- Non-binding third-party decision
- Mediation
- Arbitration
- Litigation

### 3. The Role of the Consultant
- Acts as first interpreter of the contract, must be impartial and independent.
- Prepares findings on contract performance, consistent with contract intent.
- Findings are presented in writing to both parties; parties have 15 days to dispute.
- Consultant use is voluntary; plaintiff usually pays fees.
- Consultant’s report helps plaintiff analyze options (cost/benefit).
- Consultant’s report may be questioned if the dispute goes to trial.

### 4. Negotiation
- Voluntary discussion between parties, with or without lawyers/representatives.
- No strict rules; best to analyze risks/costs/benefits beforehand.
- Pros: Parties control process/results, non-binding, inexpensive, quick.
- Cons: Voluntary (can’t force participation), hard to find a neutral facilitator.

## Summary: Pages 11–20

### 5. Non-binding Decision of a Third Party
- Disputing parties may use an independent third party (referee/adjudicator) to facilitate negotiations.
- Allows for a quick plan of action; final legal decisions can be delayed.
- Decisions are non-binding.

### 6. Mediation
- Facilitated negotiation by a neutral, trained third party (mediator).
- Mediator helps parties reach agreement but does not make decisions.
- Focuses on problem-solving and relationship building.
- Settlement only if all parties agree.

#### Advantages:
- Higher success rate with trained mediator.
- Cheaper/faster than arbitration/litigation.
- Process is private; results don’t set legal precedent.
- Opportunity for creative solutions.
- Can narrow differences even if unsuccessful.
- Parties retain some control.

#### Disadvantages:
- No guarantee of resolution; results not binding.
- More expensive than negotiation.
- Can expose weaknesses in parties’ positions.

### 7. Arbitration
- Court-like but less formal, binding on both parties.
- Private process, arbitrator is agreed upon or court-appointed.
- Adversarial in nature, governed by the B.C. Commercial Arbitration Act.

#### Advantages:
- Can be less expensive/faster than litigation (for simple cases).
- Arbitrator can be selected.
- Private process.
- Costs can be recovered or split.
- Decision is binding.

#### Disadvantages:
- Can take as long as a trial; arbitrator’s fees are high.
- Third parties cannot be added.
- Limited ability to appeal.
- Risk of incomplete disclosure (“ambush”).
- Arbitrator may lack legal/technical expertise.
- Possible unsatisfactory outcomes; some cases (e.g., Builders Liens) not eligible.
- Witnesses may be less truthful.

## Summary: Pages 21–30

### 8. Litigation
- Litigation involves the court system to resolve disputes; should be a last resort.
- In Canada, most cases are judge-only, follow formal rules, are adversarial, expensive, and time-consuming.

#### Advantages:
- May be the only way to enforce rights.
- Judge is impartial and legally trained.
- Public proceedings, third parties can be added.
- Full disclosure (no ambush), broad rights of appeal.

#### Disadvantages:
- No privacy, cannot select judge.
- High time and cost, judges may lack technical expertise.
- Can harm relationships/reputation, sets precedent.
- Litigation risk, witness credibility issues.

### Steps of Litigation:
1. **Pleadings:** Statement of facts/claims/defenses; strict time limits; terms: Plaintiff, Defendant, Third Party.
2. **Discovery:** Collect case details, interrogate individuals, exchange documents/reports, examinations under oath.
3. **Pre-trial Disposition:** Summary judgment/trial, mini-trial, settlement conference (judge as mediator).
4. **Trial:** Opening statements, evidence presentation (witnesses, documents, exhibits), closing statements, judgment.
5. **Appeal:** On law or significant factual error; no witnesses, just transcripts/exhibits.
6. **Costs:** Successful party recovers costs (30–40% of fees), costs may be split or increased for bad behavior.

### Avoiding Litigation:
- Use fair contracts (with dispute resolution clauses).
- Build strong relationships.
- Make timely, fact-based decisions.
- Be fair and keep good records.
- Follow procedures and seek help when needed.
